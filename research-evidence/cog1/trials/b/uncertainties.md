# Uncertainties and limits (round 2)

This file records what the pack does **not** establish, why it matters, and how the plan handles it. The list is deliberately longer than the certainties: the plan is a judgement under incomplete evidence, and the board should be able to see the seams.

The round-2 material changes four things: the lift is more expensive and slower, a new licensing condition caps evening events at 60, the estate awareness picture is more nuanced, and the Youth Studio's supply constraint is confirmed in writing. The uncertainties below reflect those changes. The café supplier change (U4) has **no programming budget effect**; the equipment refresh is no longer available this quarter, so it is removed from the option set rather than treated as a live uncertainty.

## A. Evidence and measurement

### A1. The 92% satisfaction figure is not representative

**What we know:** 285/310 respondents (92%) were satisfied or very satisfied. The survey was handed out in person at the free-entry Family Saturday weekend of 1–2 March 2025.

**What we do not know:** how non-visitors, weekday audiences, evening audiences, disabled visitors, or priority-estate residents feel. The sample was self-selected, 71% travelled by car, 78% attended with under-12s, and the marketing volunteer explicitly notes that non-visitors and weekday/evening audiences were not surveyed.

**Why it matters:** the board's first question — can we stop worrying about audience development? — depends on this. The answer is no. A high satisfaction score among current Saturday attendees is compatible with low reach and unmet demand elsewhere.

**How the plan handles it:** it does not use 92% as a baseline for the pilot. It sets a separate pilot experience target (≥90%, reported by segment) and treats the existing figure as context only.

**What would change the decision:** a representative borough-wide or non-visitor survey showing high satisfaction and low unmet demand across the estates. The new 120-household doorstep survey is a useful start but is not that survey; it is small and measures stated barriers, not satisfaction or behaviour.

### A2. There is no centre-level neighbourhood or equalities baseline

**What we know:** `attendance.csv` has programme, month, sessions, visits, and monthly unique participants. It has no postcode, estate, disability, age, gender, or ethnicity field. The survey has no estate or equalities breakdown. The new doorstep survey gives an estate-level household baseline: 48% never heard, 37% heard but never attended, 15% attended at least once.

**What we do not know:** which of the centre's programmes currently reach Northside and Kiln Field, in what numbers, or whether the 2023 pilot's "majority of new evening attendees" is still true. The 50% estate-share target is a **planning target** derived from the 2023 qualitative statement, not a measured programme baseline. The doorstep survey's 15% "attended at least once" does not say which programme, when, or whether they still attend.

**Why it matters:** the council requires equalities monitoring for the priority neighbourhoods, and the board's fifth question cannot be answered without it. A pilot that cannot say who came cannot prove it reached the intended audience.

**How the plan handles it:** registration collects postcode/estate, access needs, travel mode, source, and first-visit status from week 1. The report includes counts and denominators, with small-cell suppression. The baseline is reported as "not measured" rather than guessed.

**What would change the decision:** if the week-1 baseline reveals a very different estate share (for example, existing evening attendees are already mostly from the estates), the 50% target may be too easy or too hard. Recalibrate once, document why, and do not move the goalposts after week 6.

### A3. The new doorstep survey is small and measures stated barriers

**What we know:** 120 households across Northside and Kiln Field; 48% never heard; 37% heard but never attended; 15% attended at least once; among those who have heard, 57% transport, 22% cost, 11% content.

**What we do not know:** the sampling method, the response rate, the margin of error, whether the 120 households are representative of either estate, or whether the 57/22/11 split is stable. The base for the barrier questions is only the roughly half who have heard of the centre, so the counts behind the percentages are small. The survey also measures **stated** barriers, not behaviour, and the equalities team says it has not tested whether awareness alone converts.

**Why it matters:** the plan uses the survey to justify the targeted mailer and to confirm transport as the dominant barrier among the aware. If the survey is unrepresentative, both conclusions weaken.

**How the plan handles it:** it treats the survey as a **directional** baseline, not a precise estimate. It pairs the mailer with registration source data so the centre can see whether awareness actually converts. It does not use the survey to set a hard attendance target.

**What would change the decision:** a larger, representative estate survey with a tested conversion question; or early registration data showing the mailer adds no reach. Either would change the comms decision.

### A4. The 2023 pilot is a single-site, 12-week, autumn natural experiment

**What we know:** evening attendance was 38% higher than the same weeks the previous year; the majority of new attendees came from the two estates; attendance fell back and the new estate attendees stopped when the minibus stopped.

**What we do not know:** whether the result would replicate in April–June, with a different programme mix, a different audience, or a different operator. There was no contemporaneous control group, and the +38% may include novelty, weather, seasonality, or other changes. The pack gives no exact counts or estate denominators.

**Why it matters:** the pilot's entire rationale rests on this precedent. The reversal after the bus stopped is strong evidence that transport was binding, but it is still one case.

**How the plan handles it:** it replicates the bundle rather than inventing a new model, sets a per-session target anchored to the 2023 effect, and reports the result as descriptive. It does not claim that the pilot will reproduce +38% exactly.

**What would change the decision:** a failed pilot (red at week 6/13) would show that the 2023 result does not transfer. That is a reason to stop, not to add more activity.

### A5. Youth Studio demand is now better evidenced but still not a full demand curve

**What we know:** sessions fell from 4/month to 2/month from June 2024; monthly visits fell, but visits per session rose from about 49–52 to 59–76 and monthly unique participants rose from 71 to 115; there is a waiting list of 40; the partner can supply up to four extra sessions a month for the same £6,500; the interview speculation that young people had "moved on" is withdrawn.

**What we do not know:** how many of the 40 would attend regularly, whether the waiting list is current and deduplicated, how many would attend more than once a month, whether the rising unique-participant count reflects new people or the same people returning, and whether the centre can provide the room and materials for four extra sessions a month. "Unique participants" is monthly and is not deduplicated across months.

**Why it matters:** the plan now funds the expansion, so the risk is delivery and room capacity, not whether to fund it. If the centre cannot support four extra sessions, the £6,500 still buys the originally quoted two, but the waiting list will not clear as fast.

**How the plan handles it:** it funds the quoted £6,500, targets up to four extra sessions a month, uses the waitlist first, and reports actual delivery. If room or materials limit delivery to two, it delivers the quoted two and reports the constraint rather than overspending.

**What would change the decision:** a room-capacity check showing only two extra sessions are possible; a waiting list that turns out to be stale; or a partner staffing shortfall. These would reduce the expansion, not the case for it.

### A6. The pack disagrees on how many bus changes the estates are from the centre

**What we know:** `constraints.md` says both estates are "two bus changes from the centre." The front-of-house interview says they are "a bus change and a walk away."

**What we do not know:** which description is current and complete, or how long the journey actually takes door to door.

**Why it matters:** the severity of the transport barrier is central to the pilot's rationale. If it is one change plus a short walk, the barrier is still real because of the 19:10 last bus, but it is less severe than the constraint document suggests. The new doorstep survey's 57% transport finding is consistent with a real barrier but does not measure journey time.

**How the plan handles it:** it uses the conservative two-change figure and does not claim a measured journey time. The bus is justified by the 19:10 last service, the 2023 reversal, and the new survey, not by the number of changes alone.

**What would change the decision:** a measured journey audit showing the estates are easily reachable by regular bus before 19:10 would weaken the case for a dedicated minibus. The pack does not contain one.

## B. Demand and participation

### B1. Demand from the estates at a weekly frequency is unproven

**What we know:** the 2023 pilot ran one evening a week (Thursday) for 12 weeks, with talks and film, and produced estate attendance with a minibus. The current Evening Talks run twice a month and average 46.5 visits per session in 2025 Q1. The new survey says 57% of aware residents name transport as the main barrier, which is consistent with latent demand.

**What we do not know:** whether estate residents will come weekly, what content they want, whether price is a barrier (22% name cost), whether the 19:15/21:30 times suit shift patterns, and whether the audience is adults, families, or young people.

**Why it matters:** the pilot could attract the existing evening audience rather than new estate residents. A green attendance number with a low estate share would not be a success for the priority-neighbourhood goal.

**How the plan handles it:** it reports attendance and estate share separately, and it treats the estate share as a primary metric, not a secondary one. It reviews content and outreach at week 6 without adding spend.

**What would change the decision:** if estate share is red while attendance is green, the pilot is reaching the wrong audience; the response is to change content/outreach or stop, not to celebrate the total.

### B2. The targeted mailer is untested

**What we know:** word of mouth is the dominant channel (64% of survey respondents); the long-standing member found out the evening talks were back only by chance; the 2022 broad print campaign did not move first-time estate attendance; the new survey says 48% of estate households have never heard of the centre.

**What we do not know:** whether a mailer to priority households will reach the right people, whether it will convert awareness into attendance, whether the household list is accurate and current, and whether the mailer's specific offer will stand out. The equalities team explicitly says it has not tested whether awareness alone converts.

**Why it matters:** a good transport offer with poor communication will underperform, and the pilot may be judged a failure when the problem was awareness. Conversely, a mailer that reaches people but does not convert is a waste of £4,200.

**How the plan handles it:** it dispatches the mailer in weeks 1–2 with a response code, records the source at registration, compares mailer-attributed new estate attendees with partner/estate-network and word-of-mouth sources, and reports the cost per attributable new estate attendee. If the mailer adds no measurable reach, it is not repeated.

**What would change the decision:** early source data showing the mailer is doing the work would justify repeating it in a future quarter; data showing it is not would stop it. A larger, tested awareness campaign is not in this plan.

### B3. The evening programme may not be the right offer for the estates

**What we know:** the 2023 pilot used talks and film. The survey's "more evening" free-text is only 12 mentions, from a family/car audience. The new doorstep survey says only 11% of aware residents name "nothing there interests me" as the main barrier, which suggests content is less of a barrier than transport or cost.

**What we do not know:** whether talks/film appeal to Northside and Kiln Field residents, whether a participatory or family offer would do better, or whether the title "Evening Talks" carries associations that put people off.

**Why it matters:** transport is necessary but not sufficient. If the content is not relevant, the bus will run empty.

**How the plan handles it:** it uses a mixed programme (talks, film, participatory sessions) and partner/community input, and reviews content at week 6. It does not commit to a single artform.

**What would change the decision:** if bus use is high but attendance is low, or if estate attendees report the content is not for them, the programme mix should change before the transport is blamed.

### B4. The 60-attendee cap biases the attendance test

**What we know:** the new building-control condition requires the accessible entrance before any evening event with more than 60 attendees is licensed. The plan therefore caps every evening event at 60. The target is ≥50 visits per session, so the cap leaves only 20% headroom.

**What we do not know:** whether demand will exceed 60, how often, and how many people will be turned away. If demand is much higher, the pilot's attendance metric will understate it, and the estate-share metric may be affected by who is admitted first.

**Why it matters:** a capped event cannot show whether demand was 60 or 160. The board could misread a capped series as the demand ceiling, or misread turn-aways as a failure.

**How the plan handles it:** it records cap hits, admissions, turn-aways, and pre-registration oversubscription; reports them alongside attendance; and treats a run of capped events as evidence of unmet demand. It does not raise the cap without the accessible entrance and licensing confirmation. It does not add a second night without a new quote and board approval.

**What would change the decision:** if the accessible entrance is restored through a separate capital route, the cap could be lifted after handover, an independent access check, and licensing confirmation. That is not assumed in this plan.

## C. Access and capital

### C1. The lift is now £27,500 with an eight-week lead, and it is not in this plan

**What we know:** the lift repair and accessible-entrance reinstatement is quoted at **£27,500** (up from £22,000), with an **eight-week lead** (up from six), and cannot be phased. The new building-control condition requires the accessible entrance before any evening event with more than 60 attendees is licensed, whether or not it uses the first floor. The three-line plan (lift + evening + bus) is **£51,500**, £3,500 over the £48,000 envelope.

**What we do not know:** whether the contractor can start in week 1 if a separate capital route is approved, whether the building condition hides additional work, whether the £27,500 is a fixed price or an estimate, whether other first-floor works (fire, ventilation, decoration) are needed before public use, and whether the works would disrupt the ground-floor hall.

**Why it matters:** the plan defers the lift, so the accessible entrance remains out, the first floor stays closed, and evening events are capped at 60. If the lift could be funded separately and completed, the access position would improve, but the plan does not rely on it. A scope increase would make the separate bid harder, not easier.

**How the plan handles it:** it removes the lift from the funded plan, states the £51,500 arithmetic openly, recommends a separate £27,500 capital bid, caps evening events at 60, and keeps the first floor closed. It does not claim the building is accessible.

**What would change the decision:** a council capital variation or separate capital contribution of at least £27,500, plus a firm eight-week programme; or a decision by the board to prioritise the lift over the transport link (Option B/C in `options.md`). The current plan assumes neither.

### C2. The capital/revenue split of the £48,000 is not stated

**What we know:** the envelope is £48,000. The lift is £27,500 capital. The finance trustee says capital and revenue cannot be swapped without a council variation. The plan commits only revenue lines (£37,500).

**What we do not know:** whether the £48,000 contains a capital allocation, a revenue allocation, or both, and in what proportions. The pack does not say. The plan's all-revenue structure avoids a swap, but it still needs the five lines to be revenue-eligible.

**Why it matters:** if the envelope is capital-only, the plan is not compatible and the board must re-plan. If it is revenue-only, the plan is compatible, but the lift cannot be charged to it without a variation.

**How the plan handles it:** the finance trustee must confirm revenue eligibility before week 1. The plan states the assumption explicitly and does not vire between capital and revenue. The lift is outside the plan as a separate capital bid.

**What would change the decision:** a capital-only envelope would force a different plan. A revenue-only envelope would confirm the current plan and rule out charging the lift to it. This is the single most important finance approval question.

### C3. Ground-floor accessibility is assumed, not confirmed

**What we know:** the accessible entrance and lift are out; first-floor public events are prohibited. The main hall holds 180 seated. The new condition does not require the accessible entrance for evening events of 60 or fewer, but that is a licensing rule, not proof that the ground floor is independently accessible.

**What we do not know:** whether the ground-floor main hall has an independent step-free route and accessible toilet, or whether the accessible entrance is the only step-free route into the building. The pack does not say.

**Why it matters:** if the accessible entrance is the only step-free route, the evening pilot cannot run accessibly before the lift is commissioned. Running it anyway would exclude disabled visitors and contradict the service-accessibility goal. The 60 cap would not fix that.

**How the plan handles it:** week-1 verification gate. If the ground floor is not independently accessible, the pilot pauses and the board re-plans. The plan does not assume the ground floor is accessible just because the 60 cap is lawful.

**What would change the decision:** confirmation that the ground floor is independently accessible would let the pilot run as planned. Confirmation that it is not would delay the pilot until the lift is live; because the lift is not funded, that would require a re-plan (Option B, or a council variation for Option C).

### C4. The first floor has no costed programme

**What we know:** the lift repair would restore the first-floor route, but the pack quotes no activity to put on the first floor.

**What we do not know:** what first-floor programme the centre would run, what it would cost, or whether there is demand for it.

**Why it matters:** the plan does not fund the lift, but even if a separate capital route restored it, there is no costed first-floor activity. The plan should not imply that fixing the lift creates a programme.

**How the plan handles it:** no first-floor public event is planned this quarter, and no first-floor activity is costed. If a separate capital route completes, any first-floor programme would need its own plan and budget.

**What would change the decision:** a costed first-floor programme with evidence of demand. The pack does not contain one.

## D. Staffing and delivery

### D1. The evening rota has no named responsible person

**What we know:** 1.5 FTE front-of-house, no events manager post, evening opening requires a named responsible person on site, and the current rota does not staff it. The £9,600 assumes existing staff are extended, not a new post.

**What we do not know:** whether existing staff can cover 13 evenings without breaching working-time rules, whether overtime/TOIL is affordable within the quote, who the named person will be, and what happens if they are ill or on leave.

**Why it matters:** without a named responsible person, the pilot cannot legally/safely run. A rota failure in week 3 would strand the bus and the audience.

**How the plan handles it:** week-1 gate. Do not start without a named person and cover arrangements for all 13 evenings. Overtime/TOIL is agreed within the 1.5 FTE envelope before the first event.

**What would change the decision:** if no cover can be agreed, the pilot does not start; the board re-plans or uses the revenue for a daytime/transport-only model (which would need a new quote).

### D2. The minibus operator's capacity, accessibility, and reliability are unknown

**What we know:** one minibus, Northside–Kiln Field loop, 19:15 and 21:30 departures, 13 weeks, £14,400. The last regular bus is 19:10.

**What we do not know:** route stops, capacity, wheelchair accessibility, driver arrangements, safeguarding, standby/no-show terms, and whether 21:30 is late enough for events ending at 21:00. Weather, traffic, and driver shortages could affect reliability.

**Why it matters:** the bus is the pilot's defining input. A no-show strands attendees and destroys trust with the estates; an inaccessible vehicle excludes disabled residents.

**How the plan handles it:** contract confirms route, times, capacity, accessibility, and standby before signing; named contact; boarding manifest; incident log; fallback agreed. Any extra cost needs board approval.

**What would change the decision:** if the operator cannot confirm accessibility or standby, the pilot should not start; the bus is not an optional extra.

### D3. The Youth Studio room and materials may not support four extra sessions a month

**What we know:** the partner can supply staff for up to four extra sessions a month for the same £6,500; the centre supplies room and materials; there is a waiting list of 40.

**What we do not know:** whether the centre has a room available four extra times a month, whether the £6,500 covers the materials for four sessions, and whether the partner's staffing is firm for the whole quarter.

**Why it matters:** the plan funds the expansion on the basis of up to four extra sessions. If only two fit, the same quote still buys the originally quoted two, but the waiting list will not clear as fast.

**How the plan handles it:** week-1 room and materials confirmation; deliver up to four where possible, with a floor of the originally quoted two; report the constraint; no overspend.

**What would change the decision:** a room-capacity check showing only two extra sessions are possible would reduce the target, not the case for the expansion.

### D4. Mailer delivery and attribution are not guaranteed

**What we know:** the mailer costs £4,200, includes printing and postage, and is addressed to priority-neighbourhood households. The plan dispatches it in weeks 1–2 with a response code.

**What we do not know:** whether the household list is accurate, whether the mailer arrives before the first events, whether people will cite it at registration, and whether the response code will be used.

**Why it matters:** the mailer is a test of targeted awareness. If it arrives late or cannot be attributed, the test is weak.

**How the plan handles it:** confirm the dispatch date before week 1; use a simple, memorable response code; record source at registration; compare mailer-attributed registrations with network and word-of-mouth sources; report the attribution rate.

**What would change the decision:** a late dispatch or a low attribution rate would make the mailer a weak test; the plan should say so rather than claim success.

### D5. Training may not be delivered before the first event

**What we know:** the training is two days, £2,800, pre-week 1, for front-of-house staff and volunteers.

**What we do not know:** staff availability, whether volunteers can attend, and whether the trainer can deliver in the pre-week-1 window.

**Why it matters:** the 60 cap, access-needs recording, and welcome quality depend on staff confidence. Training after the first event would leave the first events under-prepared.

**How the plan handles it:** schedule before week 1; if delayed, record it and catch up before week 2; the named responsible person must be trained before the first event.

**What would change the decision:** if training cannot be delivered before the first event, delay the first event or re-plan the training date; do not run the access-critical events without it.

## E. Finance and next quarter

### E1. The next-quarter cut is verbal and unconfirmed

**What we know:** the council has verbally indicated that the following quarter may be reduced by up to 15%; nothing is confirmed and no figure is agreed. A 15% cut would leave £40,800.

**What we do not know:** whether the cut will happen, its size, its timing, or whether it applies to capital, revenue, or both.

**Why it matters:** the plan commits £37,500 of revenue for 13 weeks. If the next quarter is cut, the current plan would fit inside £40,800 if the quotes hold, with £3,300 headroom — but the lift would not, and any new activity would be squeezed.

**How the plan handles it:** no new post; revenue lines end at week 13; the mailer and training are one-off; the lift is a separate capital bid. The plan documents the "safe to stop" analysis and does not assume continuation.

**What would change the decision:** a confirmed cut would force an explicit choice between transport and other activity. The 2023 evidence says stopping the bus ends the estate reach; the board should not pretend otherwise.

### E2. The £10,500 headroom is still thin relative to the lift, but ample relative to the revenue lines

**What we know:** the quoted costs total £37,500, leaving £10,500. The quotes are estimates from suppliers and staff.

**What we do not know:** whether the bus, evening opening, youth sessions, mailer, or training will come in on quote; whether accessibility adjustments, standby, or extra cleaning will add cost.

**Why it matters:** an overrun above £10,500 needs board approval and possibly a council variation. The plan can absorb a moderate overrun, but not a major scope increase.

**How the plan handles it:** weekly spend tracker; finance trustee oversight; no virement; any overrun goes to the board before commitment. The £10,500 is not presented as a slush fund, and it is not enough for the lift.

**What would change the decision:** a firm fixed-price bus contract and a confirmed training/mailer cost would reduce the risk. The pack does not contain them.

### E3. The plan leaves £10,500 uncommitted

**What we know:** £10,500 is unallocated because no quoted line fits. The lift is £27,500 and cannot be phased; a part-share would not buy a usable repair; print is rejected; café is unavailable.

**What we do not know:** whether the board will accept under-spending, or whether it will require a written variation to commit the full envelope.

**Why it matters:** the operating constraint says the quarter budget must be committed at the start and unspent funds are not carried. The plan commits £37,500 and returns £10,500; it does not invent a line to absorb it. This is a larger headroom than round 1's £2,000, and the board may reasonably ask why.

**How the plan handles it:** the headroom is disclosed as uncommitted, not disguised as an activity. The reason is the lift's £27,500 quote: the only way to commit more would be to fund the lift, which would require dropping the bus and evening opening, or to buy print/café, which the evidence and availability rule out. If the board requires full commitment, it must approve a written variation or a smaller-scope quote, which the pack does not provide.

**What would change the decision:** a written quote for a ≤£10,500 activity that advances the plan (for example, additional transport or access support) would allow more commitment. The pack has none. A council capital variation for the lift would change the whole shape of the plan.

### E4. The plan is all revenue, which is a strength and a dependency

**What we know:** all five funded lines are revenue. The plan does not require a capital/revenue swap.

**What we do not know:** whether the £48,000 envelope is revenue-eligible for all five lines, or whether some are expected to be capital.

**Why it matters:** if the envelope is revenue-only, the plan is compatible. If it is capital-only, the plan is not. If it is mixed, the finance trustee must confirm the revenue share.

**How the plan handles it:** the finance trustee confirms revenue eligibility before week 1. The lift is a separate capital bid.

**What would change the decision:** a capital-only envelope would force a re-plan; a mixed envelope might allow the lift to be co-funded with a council variation, which the plan does not assume.

## F. Evaluation and ethics

### F1. The evaluation is small, self-selected, and uncontrolled

**What we know:** the pilot is 13 weeks, single-site, with no control group. Attendance and survey respondents are self-selected. April–June is a different season from the 2023 autumn pilot.

**What we do not know:** how much of any change is due to the pilot, the season, novelty, the mailer, the youth expansion, or other events. A positive result would not prove causation on its own.

**Why it matters:** the board may be tempted to overclaim a green result and lock in the model. The report should describe, not prove.

**How the plan handles it:** pre-registered definitions, same-weeks 2024 comparison, per-session baseline, counts and denominators, explicit limitations, no causal language beyond "consistent with".

**What would change the decision:** a comparison area or a longer run would strengthen the evidence. The pack does not provide one, and the plan does not invent one.

### F2. Data collection creates privacy and small-cell risks

**What we know:** the plan collects postcode/estate and access needs, which are personal data. The pack is synthetic and contains no personal data.

**What we do not know:** the centre's existing privacy notice, retention policy, data controller arrangements, or how small the estate subgroups will be.

**Why it matters:** collecting equalities data without consent or safeguards is a compliance and trust risk. Reporting small cells could identify individuals.

**How the plan handles it:** minimum data, purpose statement, consent, secure storage, retention period, aggregate reporting, small-cell suppression (fewer than five). A privacy notice is a week-1 gate.

**What would change the decision:** if the centre cannot put the safeguards in place, the equalities monitoring should be redesigned (for example, anonymous estate self-identification) before launch.

### F3. The deferred items have real opportunity costs

**What we know:** the plan does not fund the lift, the borough print campaign, or the café refresh. It does fund the youth expansion, mailer, and training.

**What we do not know:** how much participation the lift would have added this quarter, or whether the board would prefer a different trade-off.

**Why it matters:** a plan is defined by what it leaves out. The lift is a genuine access need, and deferring it has a cost for disabled visitors and first-floor use. The plan should not minimise that.

**How the plan handles it:** the trade-off is explicit in `options.md` and `budget.md`; the lift is a separate capital bid, not forgotten; the 60 cap and first-floor closure are reported; the board can choose Option B or C if it disagrees.

**What would change the decision:** additional capital funding, a council variation, or a board decision to prioritise the lift over transport would change the package. The plan is a judgement, not the only possible allocation.

### F4. The 60 cap and mailer attribution limit what the evaluation can claim

**What we know:** evening attendance is capped at 60; the mailer is attributed by self-reported source, not randomised exposure.

**What we do not know:** how much demand the cap suppresses, whether the people turned away are disproportionately from the priority estates, and whether mailer recipients would have attended anyway.

**Why it matters:** a capped, self-selected pilot can produce a misleadingly precise percentage. The board should see counts, denominators, cap hits, and attribution limits.

**How the plan handles it:** report cap hits and turn-aways; report source mix and cost per attributable new estate attendee; avoid causal claims; state the limitations in the council report.

**What would change the decision:** a randomised mailer test or a larger comparison area would strengthen attribution. The pack does not provide one.

## G. What would make this plan wrong

The plan should be revisited if any of the following occurs:

1. The ground-floor main hall is not independently accessible, so the pilot cannot run before the lift is live.
2. No named responsible person can be rostered for 13 evenings.
3. The minibus operator cannot confirm accessibility, capacity, or standby.
4. The finance trustee confirms the envelope is capital-only, so the revenue lines cannot be charged to it.
5. The council approves a £3,500 capital variation, making the access-complete Option C affordable and changing the trade-off.
6. The council confirms a next-quarter cut that makes the revenue lines unaffordable.
7. The mailer adds no measurable new estate reach, so the awareness test fails.
8. The Youth Studio cannot deliver even the originally quoted two extra sessions a month.
9. The week-6 data show attendance and estate share are red; the committed quarter still completes, but the recommendation changes to stop.
10. The 60 cap is hit so often that the pilot cannot test the 50/session hypothesis; the report should say the test was constrained, not that demand was 60.

None of these is a reason to hide the uncertainty. They are the conditions under which the board should change the plan rather than defend it.
