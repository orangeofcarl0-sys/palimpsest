# Assessment: what the evidence supports, and what it does not

**Venue:** Riversdale Community Arts Centre (fictional; synthetic pack)
**Planning period:** one quarter of 13 weeks, beginning the first week of April 2025
**Resource envelope:** £48,000, committed at the start, no carry-over
**Round:** 2 — revised in the light of the estate survey, the revised lift quote, the partner's Youth Studio clarification and the café notice
**Purpose:** improve participation and service accessibility, especially for the Northside and Kiln Field priority neighbourhoods

---

## 1. Decision context

This assessment separates what the source pack measures from what it only appears to measure. Five findings drive the current plan:

1. **Youth Studio visits fell because the number of sessions fell, not because demand collapsed.** Per-session attendance and monthly unique participants both rose. The partner has now confirmed in writing that its own funding change caused the reduction from four sessions a month to two, that unique participants rose from 71 in January 2024 to 115 in March 2025, and that it holds a waiting list of 40.
2. **The 92% satisfaction figure is a Family Saturday finding, not a venue-wide finding.** The survey has a narrow, self-selected sample and no non-attender data.
3. **Awareness is not uniform across the priority estates.** A new council doorstep survey of 120 households found that 48% had never heard of the centre, 37% had heard of it but never attended, and 15% had attended at least once. Among those who had heard of it, 57% named getting home after an evening event as the main barrier, 22% named cost and 11% said nothing interested them.
4. **Transport remains the dominant stated barrier among people who know the centre exists.** The 2023 evening pilot worked with a late minibus and stopped working when the minibus stopped; that is still the closest thing in the pack to a tested causal claim. The new estate survey is consistent with it, although it does not prove that awareness plus transport will convert to attendance.
5. **The building constraint has tightened.** The lift and accessible entrance are still out of service. The lift repair now costs **£27,500** (not £22,000), has an **8-week** lead time (not six), and the council has added a condition: the accessible entrance must be operable before **any evening event with more than 60 attendees is licensed**, whether or not the event uses the first floor. The current plan therefore runs a ground-floor evening pilot with a **hard cap of 60 attendees**; it does not fund the lift.

The current plan is a capped replication of the strongest local intervention, plus the now-confirmed Youth Studio supply correction, a targeted estate mailer and front-of-house training. It does not fund the lift, the borough print campaign or the café refresh.

## 2. Attendance: where participation actually is

The attendance file runs from January 2024 to March 2025. The most useful comparison for the coming quarter is April–June 2024 (the same season) against January–March 2025 (the current run rate).

| Programme | Apr–Jun 2024 visits (sessions) | Jan–Mar 2025 visits (sessions) | Visits/session Apr–Jun 2024 | Visits/session Jan–Mar 2025 | Reading |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Family Saturdays | 1,807 (15) | 1,441 (13) | 120.5 | 110.9 | Core volume; seasonal and session-count sensitive |
| Youth Studio | 524 (10) | 444 (6) | 52.4 | 74.0 | Visits down because sessions fell; per-session demand up |
| Community Choir | 749 (12) | 749 (12) | 62.4 | 62.4 | Stable |
| Access Cinema | 258 (6) | 280 (6) | 43.0 | 46.7 | Stable, slight increase |
| Evening Talks | 219 (6) | 279 (6) | 36.5 | 46.5 | Recovering after the 2024 pause |
| **All programmes** | **3,557 (49)** | **3,193 (43)** | — | — | Fewer sessions, not necessarily fewer people |

### 2.1 Family Saturdays is the volume base, not the problem

Family Saturdays is by far the largest programme: 1,807 visits in April–June 2024 and 1,441 in January–March 2025. It fluctuates with season and with whether the month has four or five sessions. Nothing in the pack suggests it needs a new intervention. It does need a **no-harm guardrail**: if a new evening offer cannibalises family attendance, the plan is a net loss. The plan therefore monitors Family Saturdays against its own seasonal baseline rather than treating it as a growth target.

### 2.2 Youth Studio: the denominator changed, and the partner has now confirmed why

This was the most important correction in round 1, and the new material removes the remaining doubt about the cause. Youth Studio ran four sessions a month until May 2024, then dropped to two sessions a month from June 2024. Monthly visits fell from 196–210 to 118–152. But:

- visits per session rose from roughly **49** (January 2024) to **76** (March 2025);
- monthly unique participants rose from **71** (January 2024) to **115** (March 2025), despite the halving of sessions;
- the partner has now written that its **own funding changed in June 2024** and it reduced delivery from four sessions a month to two; that is "the whole reason the monthly visit figure fell";
- the partner holds a **waiting list of 40** and can supply staff for **up to four extra sessions a month for the coming quarter at the same £6,500**, using the waitlist first.

The youth worker's interview speculation that "young people have moved on" has been withdrawn by the same organisation. The reading is now firmly **supply-constrained**, not demand-constrained. That does not prove that every added session will fill, but it makes additional sessions the right test and removes the earlier uncertainty about whether the partner could staff them. Round 1 planned two additional sessions a month (12 total sessions); the current quote buys up to **four additional sessions a month**, or **12 additional sessions** over the quarter and **18 sessions in total** (six a month), at the same £6,500.

### 2.3 Evening Talks: a pause and a communication failure

Evening Talks ran twice a month until June 2024, disappeared entirely in July and August 2024, returned to twice a month from September to November 2024, ran once in December 2024, and has run twice a month since January 2025. Attendance per session has recovered: 44.0 in January 2025, 46.0 in February, 49.5 in March. The long-standing member's account explains part of the damage: the programme stopped without notice, he assumed it had ended, and he only discovered it was back by chance. The survey's free-text theme "didn't know you ran other things" (6 mentions) points in the same direction, though it is a small signal.

This is a **communication and reactivation** problem as much as a programming problem. It is not evidence that evening demand is absent. The new estate survey adds a second communication fact: half of the estate households surveyed had never heard of the centre at all, so the communication gap is not only about lapsed attendees.

### 2.4 Community Choir and Access Cinema are stable

Both are steady at 12 and 6 sessions per quarter respectively. There is no evidence-based case for intervening in either this quarter. They are useful as comparison programmes: if total attendance moves while these stay flat, the change is more likely to be coming from the pilot.

## 3. The 2025 satisfaction survey: high satisfaction, narrow sample

### 3.1 What was done

The 2025 Visitor Satisfaction Survey was handed to visitors at **Family Saturdays sessions across the free-entry weekend of 1–2 March 2025**. It returned **310 completed forms**. The summary reports 92% satisfied or very satisfied (285/310), 89% would recommend, 87% say the programme meets their needs, 64% heard about the centre by word of mouth, 71% travelled by car, and 78% attended with children under 12.

Free-text themes: friendly staff (81 mentions), good value (57), parking (44), "wish there was more on in the evening" (12), café (9), "didn't know you ran other things" (6).

### 3.2 What it supports

Among the people who responded — Family Saturday visitors on one free-entry family weekend — satisfaction is high, staff and value are clear strengths, and parking is a real friction. The evening-interest and "didn't know" mentions are consistent with the interview evidence and worth testing, but they are small numbers.

### 3.3 What it does not support

The survey cannot support any of the following claims:

- **"92% of visitors are satisfied."** The 310 respondents are not a probability sample of visitors, let alone of the borough. There is no visitor denominator and no response rate, so non-response bias is unknown.
- **"Satisfaction is high and stable" across the venue.** The survey covers one programme, on one weekend, at a family event. It does not cover weekday, evening, Access Cinema, Youth Studio or Choir audiences.
- **"We can stop worrying about audience development."** Satisfaction among current attenders says nothing about people who do not attend, or about the access barriers that keep them away.
- **Demand for evening programming.** Twelve mentions out of 310 is a signal to test, not a mandate. The 2023 pilot is much stronger evidence.
- **Reach into Northside and Kiln Field.** The survey has no neighbourhood field. It cannot say which programmes reach the priority estates.
- **The needs of disabled visitors, people without children, or non-car users.** The sample is 78% families with under-12s and 71% car travellers, so it over-represents car-using families and under-represents public-transport-dependent residents — the group the bus pilot is meant to reach.

The marketing volunteer's own note at the bottom of the file says this directly: the survey covers "the people who come on a Saturday," did not survey people who do not come, and did not include the weekday and evening audiences because the free-entry weekend was a family event. The plan takes that limitation seriously.

**Implication:** use the survey as a narrow satisfaction and strength check, not as a needs assessment or a reason to defer audience development. The 92% figure should not appear as a plan target.

## 4. The new estate survey: a useful but limited non-attender baseline

### 4.1 What it found

A doorstep survey of **120 households** across Northside and Kiln Field, completed last month by the council's equalities team, reports:

| Finding | Share |
|---|---:|
| Have never heard of the centre | **48%** |
| Have heard of it but never attended | **37%** |
| Have attended at least once | **15%** |
| Of those who have heard of it, say "getting home after an evening event" is the main barrier | **57%** |
| Of those who have heard of it, say cost is the main barrier | **22%** |
| Of those who have heard of it, say "nothing there interests me" | **11%** |

The council's covering note says: "Awareness is not uniform. Half of the estates have never heard of you. Among those who have, transport home is the dominant stated barrier, not programme content. We have not tested whether awareness alone would convert to attendance."

### 4.2 What it changes

This is the first non-attender evidence in the pack. It does three things:

1. **It qualifies the board's 2022 conclusion that "awareness exists; the barrier is not awareness."** Awareness is not uniform: 48% of surveyed estate households had never heard of the centre. The 2022 borough-wide campaign still found no measurable change in first-time attendance, so the failure was not necessarily a failure to raise awareness; it was a failure to convert. But the 2022 minute should not be read as evidence that awareness is already high across the estates.
2. **It reinforces the transport diagnosis.** Among people who know the centre exists, getting home after an evening event is the dominant stated barrier (57%), well ahead of cost (22%) and programme content (11%). That is consistent with the 2023 pilot and the 19:10 last bus.
3. **It justifies a targeted estate mailer, but not as a stand-alone conversion strategy.** The mailer can address the 48% who have never heard, but the council explicitly says it has not tested whether awareness alone converts. The plan therefore pairs the mailer with the bus and a specific evening offer, and measures awareness and attendance separately.

### 4.3 What it does not establish

- **It is a doorstep survey of 120 households, not a probability sample of the estates.** The pack does not give the sampling frame, response rate or weighting. Treat the percentages as indicative, not precise.
- **The barrier percentages are based on the 52% who had heard of the centre** — about 62 households, of which 57% is roughly 35 households. A small base means the 57% figure could move substantially with a different sample.
- **It measures household awareness and stated barriers, not attendance by programme.** It cannot answer "which programmes actually reach the estates?" That still needs programme-level postcode data.
- **It does not test conversion.** Nothing in the survey shows that a household that becomes aware will attend, or that the bus will be used. Those remain hypotheses for the quarter.

## 6. Prior interventions: what has already been tested

### 6.1 The 2023 evening pilot (the strongest evidence in the pack)

**What was done:** the centre opened on Thursday evenings for talks and film for 12 weeks, and a paid late minibus ran from Northside and Kiln Field to the centre, returning after the event.

**What happened:** evening attendance was **38% higher** than the same weeks the previous year; the **majority of new evening attendees** came from the two priority estates; the programme was popular; staff reported no incidents.

**How it ended:** the minibus funding ran out. The centre continued the evening programme for six more weeks without the minibus. Attendance fell back to roughly the pre-pilot level, and the new estate attendees stopped coming. The evening programme was then suspended.

**What it means:** the evening programme was not the binding constraint; **transport was**. The front-of-house coordinator's line — "The evenings worked. They stopped working when the bus stopped. We tested that by accident" — is the cleanest causal statement in the pack. It is still a single-site, 12-week historical comparison without a control group, and "38%" and "majority" are not independently verified. But it is far stronger than the survey or the interviews, and it is directly about the priority neighbourhoods.

**Planning consequence:** do not repeat the 2023 ending. If the plan funds evening opening, it must fund the late bus for the same weeks, and it must have a week-13 decision rule for what happens when the bus funding ends. The current plan also has to respect the new licensing cap: it can test the model only up to 60 attendees a night, not the full 180-seat hall.

### 6.2 The 2022 print and poster campaign (the clearest "do not repeat")

**What was done:** £9,000 on borough-wide posters and leaflets, plus a mail-drop, for one quarter.

**What happened:** total attendance was within normal seasonal variation. The post-campaign survey found word of mouth remained the dominant channel (64%). There was **no measurable change in first-time attendance from the priority estates**.

**What the board concluded:** awareness exists; the barrier is not awareness. The board minuted: *"Do not repeat the poster spend without a specific audience and a specific offer."*

**What it means now:** the £9,000 borough print line is still not fundable on the evidence. But the new estate survey changes how the 2022 conclusion should be read. The 2022 campaign did not establish that awareness was uniformly high; it established that a borough-wide print and mail-drop did not convert. Half of the estate households in the new survey had never heard of the centre, so a **targeted mailer to priority-neighbourhood households**, tied to a specific offer (Thursday evenings, talks/film, bus times), is a different intervention and is the only targeted comms item quoted. The 2022 package also included a mail-drop, so the mailer's effect is **unproven**. It needs a pre/post test and a kill criterion, not a presumption of success. The mailer is a complement to the bus, not a substitute for it.

| Last weekday bus from the centre at 19:10; two bus changes from the estates; no staffed car park after 20:00 | Evening events after 19:00 are effectively inaccessible by public transport without the minibus. The bus subsidy is the only quoted transport fix. |

The new licence condition is the single most consequential change to the operating constraints. Round 1 assumed that only first-floor events were prohibited and that the main hall's 180 seats were available. Under the current condition, an evening event above 60 attendees cannot be licensed while the accessible entrance is out. The plan therefore treats 60, not 180, as the pilot's licensed capacity.

## 8. Evidence gaps: what we cannot conclude

The pack is rich enough to choose a direction, but it does not answer several questions the board asked. These gaps are carried into `uncertainties.md` and shape the measurement plan:

- **No programme-level neighbourhood or equalities breakdown.** The new estate survey gives household awareness and stated barriers, but not attendance by programme. Open question 5 ("Which programmes actually reach Northside and Kiln Field?") still cannot be answered from current records. This quarter must establish the programme-level baseline, not invent one.
- **The estate survey is a small doorstep sample.** It is useful for direction and as a baseline for a comparable follow-up, but it is not a precise population estimate, and the barrier percentages rest on a small aware base.
- **No conversion evidence.** The council says it has not tested whether awareness alone converts. The mailer may raise awareness without raising attendance; the bus is the conversion mechanism being tested.
- **No disabled-visitor data.** The accessible entrance and lift are out, but the pack does not say how many people are affected or whether ground-floor step-free access is independently available. The 60 cap is a licensing rule, not an accessibility solution.
- **Youth Studio capacity is now confirmed, but take-up is not.** The partner can supply up to four extra sessions a month and has a waiting list of 40. Whether all 12 additional sessions fill at the current per-session rate, and whether they cannibalise existing sessions, is still a test.
- **No bus capacity, route, stop, insurance or accessibility details.** The quote gives a loop and two departure times, not an operating specification.
- **No specific-offer awareness baseline.** The estate survey gives general awareness, not knowledge of a Thursday evening programme. The plan must establish the offer baseline in week 1.
- **No confirmed 15% cut.** The council's indication is verbal; neither the basis (envelope vs plan) nor the timing is agreed.
- **No quoted cost for programming, backfill, external survey or access audit.** The plan assumes existing staff/volunteer capacity and existing programme formats; if that is wrong, those elements cannot be committed this quarter.

## 9. Answers to the board's five open questions

1. **"Satisfaction is 92%. Can we stop worrying about audience development?"**
   No. 92% is the share of 310 responding Family Saturday visitors on one free-entry weekend, not of all visitors or the borough. It is compatible with high satisfaction among current family attenders and significant unmet access demand among non-attenders. The new estate survey makes the gap concrete: 48% of surveyed estate households had never heard of the centre, and 37% had heard of it but never attended. Satisfaction among current attenders cannot answer the audience-development question.

2. **"Youth Studio visits fell. Are young people losing interest?"**
   No. The partner has confirmed that its own funding change reduced delivery from four sessions a month to two; that is the whole reason visits fell. Visits per session rose from about 49 to 76 and monthly unique participants rose from 71 to 115, with a waiting list of 40. The youth worker's "moved on" speculation has been withdrawn. Additional sessions are the right test, and the current £6,500 now buys up to four extra sessions a month.

3. **"The 2023 evening pilot worked. Why did it stop working?"**
   The minibus funding ended. The evening programme continued for six weeks without transport; attendance fell back to pre-pilot levels and the new estate attendees stopped coming. Transport was the binding constraint, not the programme. The new estate survey is consistent: among people who know the centre, 57% name getting home after an evening event as the main barrier.

4. **"The council may cut us next quarter. Which parts are safe to stop?"**
   The targeted mailer is the least proven and can be stopped first; the two-day training is a one-off and can be deferred; the additional Youth Studio sessions can revert to the existing two a month. The late bus and the named responsible person are not safe to stop without losing the goal, because the 2023 evidence shows the estate attendance gains reverse when the bus goes. The plan is costed at £37,500, so it still fits a 15% cut to the £48,000 envelope (£40,800); a 15% cut applied to the plan itself would require dropping the mailer and training to reach £30,500. The lift is not in the revenue plan at all; its new £27,500 cost and 8-week lead are a separate capital decision.

5. **"Which programmes actually reach the Northside and Kiln Field estates?"**
   We still do not know at programme level. The attendance data have no neighbourhood field and the 2025 satisfaction survey did not ask. The new estate survey tells us that 15% of surveyed households have attended at least once, but not which programme they attended. This quarter establishes a postcode/equalities baseline at every funded activity, with the council's quarterly equalities report as the output. The 2023 pilot suggests evening-plus-bus reaches the estates, but that is a prior, not a current programme-level measurement.

## 10. Bottom line

The best-evidenced participation lever is **evening opening with a late minibus for the two priority estates**, now operated within a **60-attendee licensing cap** because the accessible entrance cannot be repaired alongside this participation plan within the envelope. The best-evidenced supply correction is **restoring and expanding Youth Studio frequency**: the same £6,500 now buys up to four additional sessions a month, and the partner has a waiting list of 40. The communication gap is real and is larger than round 1 assumed — half of surveyed estate households have never heard of the centre — but the solution must still be **targeted and tied to a specific offer**, not a repeat of the borough print campaign. The lift is an unresolved **capital and access** issue, now £27,500 with an 8-week lead; it cannot be absorbed into this £48,000 revenue plan, and adding it to the recommended portfolio would cost £65,000. The recommended portfolio, its alternatives and its costs are set out in `options.md` and `budget.md`.
