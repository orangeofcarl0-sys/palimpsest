# Uncertainties and evidence gaps

**Round:** 2 — revised for the estate survey, the revised lift quote, the Youth Studio clarification and the café notice

This file records what the plan does **not** know, how that affects the plan, and what would resolve it. It is deliberately not a list of generic risks; each item is a specific gap in the source pack or a specific assumption the plan is making.

The plan is designed to be honest about these gaps rather than to pre-solve them. Where a number does not exist, the plan establishes a baseline rather than inventing one. Where a cost is not quoted, the plan does not commit it. Where a new condition makes an earlier design non-compliant, the plan changes the design and says so.

---

## 1. The satisfaction survey is not a population estimate

**What we know:** 310 Family Saturday visitors on the free-entry weekend of 1–2 March 2025 returned forms; 92% were satisfied or very satisfied (285/310); 78% attended with children under 12; 71% travelled by car; 64% heard by word of mouth.

**What we do not know:** the visitor denominator, the response rate, the non-response bias, or the views of non-attenders, weekday/evening audiences, priority-estate residents, disabled visitors, young people or people without children. The survey was not weighted and covered one programme on one weekend.

**Why it matters:** the 92% figure cannot be used as a venue-wide satisfaction measure, a needs assessment, or a reason to stop audience development. It also cannot tell us whether the evening programme is wanted by the estates; the 12 evening mentions are a small, family-heavy signal.

**How it is handled:** the plan uses the survey only for what it supports — high satisfaction among responding Family Saturday visitors, staff/value strengths, a parking friction, and small signals worth testing. It does not set a satisfaction target and does not use 92% as a baseline for the pilot.

**What would resolve it:** a non-attender/estate survey with a proper sample frame and response-rate reporting. The council has now provided a small doorstep survey, but it is not a substitute for a programme-level attendance baseline.

## 2. There is still no programme-level neighbourhood or equalities baseline

**What we know:** the attendance file has no postcode, neighbourhood or demographic field. The 2025 satisfaction survey did not ask. The council's new estate survey gives household awareness and attendance-at-least-once, but not attendance by programme. The council requires a quarterly report with attendance and equalities monitoring for the priority neighbourhoods.

**What we do not know:** which programmes currently reach Northside and Kiln Field, in what numbers, or whether the estates are represented proportionally. Open question 5 ("Which programmes actually reach the estates?") still cannot be answered from existing records. The estate survey says 15% of surveyed households have attended at least once, but not which programme they attended.

**Why it matters:** the pilot's primary equity target — at least 50% of new evening attendees from the two estates — has no programme-level baseline. It is a planning threshold based on the 2023 pilot's "majority," not a measured starting point. If postcode capture is poor, the target could be met or missed on a biased denominator.

**How it is handled:** postcode capture starts in week 1, is optional and aggregated, and reports the "unknown postcode" share. Northside and Kiln Field are reported separately. The 50% target is labelled a planning threshold, not a forecast. The first quarter's programme-level data becomes the baseline for the next.

**What would resolve it:** a consistent postcode/equalities collection protocol agreed with the council, and a full quarter of programme-level data. If the council has standard categories, use them rather than inventing new ones.

## 3. The new estate survey is small, and it does not test conversion

**What we know:** a doorstep survey of **120 households** across Northside and Kiln Field found 48% had never heard of the centre, 37% had heard but never attended, and 15% had attended at least once. Among those who had heard, 57% named getting home after an evening event as the main barrier, 22% cost and 11% programme content. The council's note says awareness is not uniform and it has not tested whether awareness alone converts.

**What we do not know:** the sampling frame, response rate, weighting or interview protocol. The barrier percentages are based on the 52% who had heard of the centre — about 62 households, of which 57% is roughly 35 households. That is a small base. The survey measures stated barriers and household awareness, not behaviour, programme attendance or conversion. It also cannot say whether the 48% never-heard group would attend if aware.

**Why it matters:** the plan uses the survey to justify a targeted mailer and to reinforce the transport diagnosis, not to prove that awareness alone will fill the evening programme. If the survey sample is unrepresentative, the 48% and 57% figures could be misleading. If awareness rises but attendance does not, the mailer will have failed on conversion even if it succeeded on recall.

**How it is handled:** the plan treats the survey as an indicative household baseline, labels it as such in reporting, and pairs the mailer with the bus and a specific offer. It measures general awareness and specific-offer awareness separately, and reports attendance and bus use as separate outcomes. It does not use the 48% figure as a programme-level denominator.

**What would resolve it:** the council's sampling method and a larger, comparable follow-up sample; and the quarter's own attendance, postcode and bus data. A conversion test would need a design that links awareness to attendance, which this quarter does not have.

## 4. Youth Studio capacity is now confirmed, but take-up and scheduling are not

**What we know:** visits per session rose from about 49 to 76, and monthly unique participants rose from 71 to 115, while sessions fell from four to two a month. The partner has now confirmed in writing that its own funding change caused the reduction, that interest has not fallen, that it holds a waiting list of 40, and that it can supply staff for **up to four extra sessions a month for the coming quarter at the same £6,500**, using the waitlist first.

**What we do not know:** whether the centre can provide the room and dates for 12 additional sessions (18 total, six a month); whether all 12 sessions fill at or near the current 74/session rate; whether the same young people attend multiple sessions; whether extra sessions cannibalise the existing two-a-month pattern; and whether "up to four" becomes four in practice.

**Why it matters:** round 1 planned two additional sessions a month and a 12-session total. The current offer is up to four additional sessions a month at the same price, so the plan now targets 18 sessions, at least 1,200 visits, at least 150 monthly unique participants by the final month and at least 65 visits/session. If the extra sessions fill more slowly, the visit target may be too high. If the room cannot support six sessions a month, the quantity is not deliverable even though the staff are available. If the partner delivers fewer than four extra sessions, the £6,500 is unchanged, so the shortfall is a value-for-money issue, not a budget saving.

**How it is handled:** the plan confirms room and dates before commitment; the target is ≥65 visits/session (below the current 74), allowing for marginal demand; the partner register records sessions, visits, unique participants and the waiting list; monthly uniques are not summed. If delivery falls short, the report says so and re-plans within the envelope.

**What would resolve it:** a written room/session schedule for all 18 sessions before week 1, plus session-level attendance and waiting-list data during the quarter.

## 5. The 2023 pilot may not replicate, and the 60 cap limits the test

**What we know:** the 2023 evening pilot, with a late minibus, produced 38% higher evening attendance than the same weeks the previous year, with most new attendees from the two estates; when the minibus funding ended, attendance fell back and the new estate attendees stopped.

**What we do not know:** whether the 2025 audience, programme, route, operator, weather, school holidays or local transport context are comparable. The 2023 result is a single-site, 12-week historical comparison without a control group; "38%" and "majority" are not independently verified. In addition, the 2023 pilot did not operate under the current 60-attendee condition. Current evening attendance is 46.5/session; a 38% uplift on that base would be about 64/session, above the cap.

**Why it matters:** the pilot is the plan's strongest evidence, but it is not a guarantee. If the gain does not replicate, the plan should say so and stop or redesign. If the cap binds, the pilot may show a full house at 60 while actually leaving demand unmet; the report must not present capped attendance as a full replication.

**How it is handled:** the pilot is a capped replication with better measurement, not an assumption of success. It uses both the same-quarter 2024 baseline (36.5/session) and the current run rate (46.5) and has explicit stop/redesign bands. Turnaways and waitlist demand are recorded. The 38% figure is a planning reference, not a forecast.

**What would resolve it:** the quarter's own per-session, cap-status and estate-share data, compared with the same weeks last year, plus bus carryings. A control area is not available; the report should state that limitation. A true test of uncapped demand requires the lift repair.

## 6. The bus quote is not a full operating specification

**What we know:** £14,400 buys one minibus on the Northside–Kiln Field loop for 13 weeks, with 19:15 and 21:30 departures, from an operator quote.

**What we do not know:** direction of each departure, exact stops and pick-up points, capacity, wheelchair accessibility, insurance and liability, safeguarding arrangements, driver responsibilities, what happens if a departure is missed, or whether the operator can guarantee 13 weeks.

**Why it matters:** the bus is the mechanism that made the 2023 pilot work. If the route, stops or times do not match residents' needs, the pilot will not test the model. If the bus is not accessible, it may exclude disabled residents. If capacity is below estate demand, the estate-share target may be limited by seats rather than demand. If the operator fails, participants may be stranded. The estate survey's 57% transport finding raises the stakes: the bus is the plan's main answer to the dominant stated barrier, so a weak operating specification would undermine the core test.

**How it is handled:** the plan treats the 19:15 estate-to-centre / 21:30 centre-to-estate pattern as a working assumption to confirm before contract. The contract must specify stops, capacity, accessibility, insurance, safeguarding and contingency. Carryings and turnaways are recorded by departure. If capacity is insufficient, the plan reports unmet demand and seeks a written quote for any extra vehicle or run; it does not assume one. If the specification cannot be agreed, the pilot does not start as designed.

**What would resolve it:** a signed operator contract and route/timetable before week 1, plus a dry-run check of stops and timing.

## 7. Thirteen evenings stretch 1.5 FTE front-of-house

**What we know:** the centre has 1.5 FTE front-of-house across the whole week, no events manager, and evening opening requires a named responsible person on site. The £9,600 evening line assumes existing staff are extended, not a new post.

**What we do not know:** the actual rota, contracted hours, overtime/TOIL arrangements, sickness cover, or whether the same staff can cover daytime and 13 evenings without unsafe hours or service loss. The 60 cap reduces crowding but does not reduce the number of evenings or the staffing requirement.

**Why it matters:** if the rota cannot be sustained, evenings will be paused or run unsafely. If daytime service suffers, the plan damages the core programme while trying to grow the evening one.

**How it is handled:** a named responsible person and backup for every evening; weekly rota/hours check; no additional evening events; pause rather than run without cover; board review at week 6. The plan does not create a new post or assume additional staff.

**What would resolve it:** a signed rota for all 13 dates before week 1, with named backups, and a weekly hours/absence check during the quarter. If cover cannot be guaranteed, reduce frequency or stop the pilot.

## 8. The lift is now more expensive, slower, and a condition of larger evening events

**What we know:** the accessible entrance and passenger lift are out of service; first-floor public events are prohibited until the lift is repaired. The contractor has re-quoted: the repair and accessible entrance reinstatement now cost **£27,500** (up from £22,000), with an **8-week** lead time (up from six), because parts must be remanufactured. The council's building control officer adds that the accessible entrance must be operable before **any evening event with more than 60 attendees is licensed**, whether or not the event uses the first floor. The lift is capital, cannot be phased, and capital/revenue cannot be swapped without a council variation.

**What we do not know:** whether ground-floor step-free access is independently available; how many visitors are affected; whether the council will release capital; whether the contractor can meet the 8-week lead; what first-floor programme would run once the lift is back; and whether the council would accept a temporary access arrangement (none is quoted, so the plan does not assume one). The lift itself cannot be phased.

**Why it matters:** the plan is ground-floor only, capped at 60, and does not restore step-free access. It would be wrong to present the pilot as fully accessible. The new condition means the 60 cap is not optional. It also changes the options arithmetic: round 1's "lift + evening + bus" fallback was £46,000 when the lift was £22,000; it is now **£51,500**, over the envelope by £3,500. The full recommended plan plus the lift is **£65,000**, over by £17,000. The headroom is £10,500, which is £17,000 short. There is no affordable package that combines the lift, the evening pilot and the bus within £48,000.

**How it is handled:** all funded activity is ground-floor; no first-floor bookings; **every evening is hard-capped at 60**, with booking and door-count reconciliation; access information is honest about the lift being out and step-free entry not being available; staff are trained to answer access questions; unmet access requests are recorded; the lift is a separate capital decision. The plan does not use the £10,500 headroom for it without a council variation.

**What would resolve it:** a capital decision and a contractor date, plus an access audit covering the entrance, lift, toilets and routes. The audit is not quoted in this pack and is not committed this quarter. If the board makes step-free access a precondition, it must choose the access-first package (lift + Youth Studio + mailer + training, £41,000, which drops the evening and bus) or seek additional capital for the £51,500 lift-plus-evening-plus-bus package. The intermediate lift + evening + Youth + training package (£46,400) is a sequencing trap: the evening cannot start until the lift is certified around week 9 if step-free access is the precondition. It cannot add the lift to the recommended plan.

## 9. The envelope's capital/revenue split is not stated

**What we know:** the envelope is £48,000, fixed, committed at the start, no carry-over. Capital and revenue cannot be swapped without a council variation. The lift is capital.

**What we do not know:** whether the £48,000 is all revenue, all capital, or a mixed envelope; whether capital is already available elsewhere; or what the council's variation process and timing are.

**Why it matters:** if the envelope is revenue-only, the lift cannot be funded from it at all. If it is mixed, the board still has to choose between the lift and the participation portfolio. The plan assumes it can commit £37,500 of revenue and no capital; that assumption should be confirmed by the finance trustee.

**How it is handled:** the budget shows £0 capital and £37,500 revenue; the lift is explicitly outside the plan; the board is asked to authorise a separate capital conversation. No virement is assumed.

**What would resolve it:** written confirmation from the finance trustee/council of the envelope's composition and the capital variation route.

## 10. The targeted mailer is still untested, and the new baseline cuts both ways

**What we know:** the 2022 borough-wide print and poster campaign, which included a mail-drop, produced no measurable change in first-time attendance from the priority estates; word of mouth remained dominant at 64%; the board minuted against repeating poster spend without a specific audience and offer. The new estate survey finds 48% of 120 surveyed households have never heard of the centre, and 57% of those who have heard name getting home after an evening event as the main barrier.

**What we do not know:** whether a current, usable address list for the two estates exists; whether a targeted mailer with a specific evening/bus offer will move awareness or attendance; whether the 48% never-heard figure will hold in the mailer recipient sample; whether awareness, if raised, will convert given the 57% transport and 22% cost barriers. The mailer may be ignored, may reach the wrong household member, or may raise expectations the bus/programme cannot meet.

**Why it matters:** £4,200 is 11.2% of the committed plan. If the mailer does not work, the plan should stop it rather than repeat it. The 2022 precedent is a warning, not a proof that all mail is useless. The +10 percentage point target is a planning threshold chosen to be detectable, not an evidence-based forecast; it should be revised if the pre-mailer baseline is very high or very low. The new 48% baseline makes the mailer more justified than round 1 assumed, but it does not prove that awareness alone converts.

**How it is handled:** the mailer is a test with a pre-mailer baseline (week 1), a post-mailer check (weeks 8 and 12), delivery confirmation and a kill criterion. It asks both general awareness and specific-offer awareness. If there is no measurable gain, it is not repeated. Free channels continue regardless. The bus, not the mailer, is the main conversion mechanism.

**What would resolve it:** a pre/post awareness measure with a reported sample and response rate, plus delivery confirmation and attendance data for mailer-recipient areas. If the sample is too small, report counts and say so rather than claiming a percentage-point effect.

## 11. The 15% council cut is unconfirmed and its basis is unclear

**What we know:** the council has **verbally** indicated that the **following** quarter may be reduced by up to 15%. Nothing is confirmed and no figure is agreed.

**What we do not know:** whether the cut will happen, whether it applies to the £48,000 envelope or to the plan, whether it is 15% or less, when it would take effect, or whether it would be accompanied by other conditions.

**Why it matters:** the plan's resilience claim depends on the reading. If the cut applies to the envelope, £37,500 fits £40,800 with £3,300 to spare. If it applies to the plan, the mailer and training would be dropped to reach £30,500. A deeper cut would put the Youth Studio extras and possibly the bus at risk. The lift is a separate capital issue; a revenue cut does not make it cheaper.

**How it is handled:** both scenarios are costed in `budget.md`; the stop/scale order protects the bus last; the plan does not assume carry-over. The board should seek written confirmation before the next quarter's commitment.

**What would resolve it:** a written council budget letter stating the amount, basis and timing.

## 12. Attendance data are monthly aggregates with undefined terms

**What we know:** the file gives month, programme, sessions, visits and unique participants. It does not define "visit" or "unique participant," and it has no session-level, demographic or neighbourhood fields.

**What we do not know:** whether visits are door counts, bookings or tickets; whether unique participants are de-duplicated within a month but not across months; whether sessions were cancelled, capped or moved; whether the same person attends multiple programmes.

**Why it matters:** per-session and unique-participant conclusions depend on these definitions. The Youth Studio correction is robust because the session count and visits move in opposite directions, but the absolute numbers may not be comparable across programmes. Monthly unique counts cannot be summed into a quarterly unique figure. The new 18-session target depends on the current 74/session rate, which may not be stable at higher frequency.

**How it is handled:** the plan uses the data for direction and per-session comparisons, not for precise population estimates. It defines new/returning status, reports monthly unique counts separately, and does not sum them. Session-level data collection starts in week 1.

**What would resolve it:** a data dictionary from the box office/attendance system and session-level records for the quarter.

## 13. Seasonality, weather and school holidays may move the numbers

**What we know:** attendance is seasonal; Family Saturdays is much higher in spring than in January–February; Evening Talks were absent in July–August 2024; the 2025 Q1 evening attendance rose from 44.0 to 49.5 per session.

**What we do not know:** how much of the Q1 evening rise is recovery from the 2024 pause versus a trend; how Easter, school holidays, weather or local events will affect April–June 2025; whether the 2023 pilot's 38% gain was itself seasonal.

**Why it matters:** a single quarter can mislead. A good April–June could be weather; a bad one could be holidays. The plan should not over-claim attribution.

**How it is handled:** the primary comparison is the same quarter last year (April–June 2024), with the current run rate as a secondary reference; monthly and rolling averages are reported; the report states seasonality as a limitation. The 38% figure is a planning reference, not a forecast.

**What would resolve it:** multiple quarters of data, or a comparison area. Neither is available in this pack.

## 14. Some necessary costs are not quoted

**What we know:** the pack quotes evening opening, bus, lift, Youth Studio extras, print, mailer, training and café equipment. It does not quote new programming/artist fees, staff backfill, external survey fieldwork, an access audit or a price subsidy.

**What we do not know:** whether existing talks/film content can fill 13 evenings without new fees; whether training can be delivered without backfill; whether the awareness survey can be run by existing staff/volunteers at acceptable quality; whether an access audit is needed before the pilot; whether existing registration processes can enforce the 60-attendee cap without a new booking system; whether cost is a big enough barrier among estate residents to require a subsidy (the estate survey says 22% of aware residents, but no subsidy is quoted).

**Why it matters:** the plan assumes existing staff/volunteer capacity and existing programme formats. If that is wrong, the activity may be under-resourced, or an unquoted cost may appear that cannot be committed this quarter. It cannot solve the 22% cost barrier with an unquoted subsidy.

**How it is handled:** the plan commits no unquoted cost; it says explicitly that new programming, backfill, external fieldwork, an access audit, a booking system and a price subsidy are outside the quarter. If any becomes necessary, it needs a written quote and a re-cut within the envelope, not an informal overspend.

**What would resolve it:** confirmation from staff/partner that existing content and capacity are sufficient, and quotes if they are not.

## 15. Parking is a real friction but not addressed

**What we know:** 44 free-text mentions of parking; 71% of survey respondents travelled by car; there is no staffed car park after 20:00.

**What we do not know:** how many evening attendees drive, whether parking availability affects their attendance, or whether the 20:00 staffing change is a deterrent. The survey's car share is from a Family Saturday sample, not an evening sample. The new estate survey is about the two priority estates, where car ownership may differ.

**Why it matters:** the pilot targets public-transport users, but car users are the majority of the surveyed Family Saturday audience. If parking is a bigger barrier than the bus for some attendees, the pilot may not reach them.

**How it is handled:** the plan is honest that it does not solve parking; the bus is for public-transport-dependent residents. Parking is noted as a next-quarter review candidate, not a costed intervention.

**What would resolve it:** an evening travel/parking survey and a parking review with the venue/council. Neither is costed here.

## 17. Data privacy and small-cell reporting

**What we know:** the plan collects postcode and equalities data for the council's quarterly report.

**What we do not know:** the council's exact equalities categories, its small-cell threshold, or its preferred anonymisation method.

**Why it matters:** postcode plus other fields can identify individuals in a small venue. Reporting estate shares from very small cells could breach privacy or produce misleading percentages. The 120-household estate survey is already a small sample; the quarter's evening and Youth Studio cells may be smaller.

**How it is handled:** collection is optional and aggregated; small cells are suppressed; no personal data is published; the council's template/categories are used where available. If the council has no standard, the plan uses its existing reporting template rather than inventing one.

**What would resolve it:** written guidance from the council on categories, thresholds and anonymisation before collection begins.

## 19. What this plan is not claiming

To avoid overclaiming in the board report:

- It is **not** claiming that 92% of all visitors are satisfied. That figure is a Family Saturday sample.
- It is **not** claiming that young people lost interest in the Youth Studio. The partner has confirmed the opposite: supply fell, unique participants rose and a waiting list of 40 exists.
- It is **not** claiming that the bus alone fixes access. It addresses the 19:10 last-bus barrier for the pilot evenings; parking, step-free access and first-floor access remain unresolved.
- It is **not** claiming that the lift is fixed or that the venue is fully accessible. It is ground-floor only, with step-free entry unavailable.
- It is **not** claiming that evening events above 60 can run. They cannot be licensed while the accessible entrance is out, and the pilot will not admit above 60.
- It is **not** claiming that the pilot can replicate the full 2023 uplift. If demand exceeds 60, the cap censors the result; turnaways are evidence of unmet demand, not a success measure.
- It is **not** claiming that the targeted mailer will work. It is a test with a kill criterion, and the 2022 borough-wide mail-drop did not convert.
- It is **not** claiming that awareness alone converts. The council has not tested that, and 57% of aware estate residents cite transport home as the main barrier.
- It is **not** claiming that the targets are forecasts. They are planning thresholds; the report will show sample sizes, ranges and unknowns.
- It is **not** claiming that a programme-level estate baseline exists. It does not; this quarter establishes it.
- It is **not** claiming that the café equipment refresh is available. It has been deferred to the next financial year; the coffee supplier change does not change the programming budget.

## 20. Residual risk statement

After the plan's mitigations, the main residual risks are:

1. **The 2023 gain does not replicate**, or the 60 cap prevents a fair test of it, and the quarter shows that transport alone is not sufficient under current conditions. The decision rule is designed to detect this and stop or redesign rather than rationalise it.
2. **The lift remains out of service**, so disabled visitors still face barriers, and evening demand above 60 cannot be served. This is an unresolved capital issue, not something the revenue plan can fix; the turnaway data are the bridge to the capital case.
3. **The estate share cannot be measured well** because postcode capture is incomplete. The plan reports the unknown share rather than imputing.
4. **The 1.5 FTE rota cannot sustain 13 evenings**, forcing a pause or a frequency reduction. The plan monitors this weekly and pauses rather than runs unsafely.
5. **The council cut is deeper than 15% or arrives mid-quarter**, forcing the stop/scale order and possibly stopping the pilot. The plan does not assume carry-over or a top-up.
6. **The mailer raises awareness but not attendance**, because transport, cost or timing still block conversion. The report should separate awareness from attendance and not claim the mailer succeeded on recall alone.

These risks are acceptable for a time-bound, measured pilot within a fixed envelope. They would not be acceptable for a permanent commitment made without the week-13 review.
